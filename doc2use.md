**Cladophialophora bantiana** is a neurotropic dematiaceous (melanized) mold and the single most common cause of cerebral phaeohyphomycosis, accounting for roughly one-third to nearly half of reported cases, with a distinctive tendency to cause brain abscess in otherwise healthy, immunocompetent hosts and a mortality of approximately 65–74% regardless of immune status.[1][2][3] It presents clinically as a slowly expanding, space-occupying brain lesion that mimics a tumor, is diagnostically challenging because CSF cultures are usually negative, and requires combined aggressive neurosurgical resection plus prolonged antifungal therapy.[1][4][2]

**Microbiology and pathogenesis**

- A phaeoid (brown-pigmented) fungus whose cell wall melanin is considered the key virulence factor, interfering with microglial defense; it has a striking, near-exclusive tropism for the CNS and is rarely isolated from other sources.[2][3]
- Found in soil worldwide; infection is thought to occur via inhalation or traumatic cutaneous inoculation, followed by hematogenous dissemination to the brain. In immunocompetent patients cerebral infection commonly occurs without any identifiable pulmonary source.[1][2][5]
- Classically considered a tropical/subtropical organism, but autochthonous and temperate-climate cases are now reported, possibly reflecting an expanding geographic range.[5]
- It is a true pathogen; laboratories should be alerted because Biosafety Level-3 precautions may be warranted when it is suspected.[2]

**Clinical presentation**

- The dominant manifestation is **brain abscess** (~97% of cerebral cases), typically a solitary lesion, though multiple/satellite lesions occur; combined brain-and-meningeal involvement (~14%) and isolated meningitis (~2.5%) are much less common.[2]
- Symptoms reflect a slowly enlarging mass: **headache, seizures, and focal/localizing neurologic deficits**, often with a subacute-to-chronic course that simulates a neoplasm.[1][4]
- Reported presentations include ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, visual field defects, and progressive cognitive/motor decline; frontal and parietal lobes are frequently involved.[6][7][8]
- Disease occurs about three times more often in men; roughly half of patients have no identifiable risk factor, while the remainder have immunosuppression (transplantation, hematologic malignancy/solid tumor, less often neutropenia).[2][3]
- Disseminated disease with concurrent skin and lung lesions can occur, particularly in transplant recipients.[5]

**Imaging**

- MRI/CT typically shows **rim-enhancing focal lesion(s)** with surrounding edema and mass effect, often with diffusion restriction on DWI, consistent with abscess; hemorrhage may be present.[4][8][9]
- Prognostically favorable features are an easily resectable, well-encapsulated mass; poor prognosis correlates with solitary lesions that cannot be fully resected, poorly demarcated borders, or multiple satellite lesions.[1]
- Progression to ependymal enhancement and ventriculitis signals compartmentalized intraventricular disease poorly controlled by systemic therapy.[9]

**Diagnostic testing**

Direct sampling of CNS tissue/abscess material for microscopy, culture, and molecular identification is the diagnostic gold standard, since serologic and CSF-based tests are unreliable.[6][10]

- **Direct microscopy/histopathology:** KOH mount and Gram stain of aspirated pus show narrow, septate, phaeoid (pigmented) hyphae with sparse branching; melanin can be confirmed with Fontana-Masson staining of tissue.[10][11][8]
- **Culture:** Grows on Sabouraud dextrose agar as slow-growing, velvety, olivaceous-black to dark colonies (often within ~5 days but classical ID can take a week or more); lactophenol cotton blue tape prep shows dark septate hyphae with long, sparsely branched wavy chains of smooth conidia.[10][8][1]
- **Molecular identification:** ITS region sequencing/BLAST confirms species; pan-fungal PCR with sequencing is recommended when microscopy/histology is positive but culture is negative, and should be done on fresh (not formalin-fixed) sterile tissue.[10][12]
- **Metagenomic/NGS testing:** Plasma or biopsy metagenomic next-generation sequencing (e.g., Karius test) has identified C. bantiana in as little as ~40 hours and can expedite diagnosis when conventional methods are slow or cultures negative.[6][10]
- **CSF/biomarkers:** CSF cultures are mostly negative; β-D-glucan may be useful but there is no specific antigen test. AFB and TB PCR are typically obtained to exclude mimics.[10][4]
- Because pulmonary involvement is common, less-invasive specimens (BAL, protected tracheal specimens, lung/skin lesion aspirates) can be screened for the organism.[10][5]

**Differential diagnosis**

Given the tumor-like radiographic appearance and nonspecific symptoms, consider:

- **Other mold brain abscesses:** Aspergillus species; Mucorales (often rhinocerebral with sinus origin); Scedosporium species/Lomentospora prolificans; Fusarium species.[4]
- **Other neurotropic dematiaceous molds:** Rhinocladiella mackenziei (Middle East), Exophiala dermatitidis, Ochroconis gallopava, Fonsecaea monophora, Cladophialophora modesta.[11][3]
- **Yeast CNS infections:** Cryptococcus (cryptococcoma/meningoencephalitis), Candida (microabscesses), other non-candida yeasts.[4]
- **Pyogenic bacterial brain abscess** and, importantly, **CNS tuberculoma/tuberculous abscess**, which share imaging and symptom features and must be actively excluded.[10]
- **Primary or metastatic brain tumor**, which this infection most closely mimics on imaging and clinically.[1][5]

**Treatment**

The mainstays are **complete surgical excision of the abscess combined with prolonged antifungal therapy and, where applicable, reversal/reduction of immunosuppression**; despite this, there is no clearly proven optimal regimen and mortality remains high.[1][10][2][5]

- **Surgery:** Craniotomy with complete mass removal (or aspiration) was performed in ~89% of reviewed cases; complete excision is associated with the best outcomes, whereas incomplete resection predicts death. Repeat surgery for progression was needed in ~20%. The 2014 ESCMID/ECMM guidelines for black-fungus (phaeohyphomycosis) disease recommend complete excision of the brain abscess, with addition of single or combination antifungals to improve survival.[1][10]
- **Antifungal agents used (from systematic review):** voriconazole (~77%), lipid amphotericin B formulations (~43%), amphotericin B deoxycholate (~31%), flucytosine/5-FC (~26%), posaconazole (~10%), fluconazole (~8%), with occasional itraconazole, isavuconazole, and echinocandins (limited CSF penetration, little success).[10]
- **Common regimen:** sequential/combination therapy — liposomal amphotericin B plus voriconazole (often with flucytosine or posaconazole), transitioning to oral azole maintenance, with duration individualized to radiologic resolution.[10][8]
- **In vitro susceptibility** supports azoles: lowest MIC90 values for posaconazole and itraconazole (0.125 µg/mL), then isavuconazole (0.5), amphotericin B (1), and voriconazole (2); fluconazole is poorly active (MIC90 64). Posaconazole is a reasonable alternative to amphotericin B and isavuconazole offers adequate activity with an IV formulation.[13]
- **Intraventricular/intrathecal antifungals** (e.g., intraventricular/intrathecal amphotericin B via Ommaya reservoir) can be considered as adjuncts in refractory ventriculitis or compartmentalized intraventricular disease when systemic therapy fails despite therapeutic serum levels.[6][10][9]
- **Monitoring and duration:** prolonged therapy (often many months) with serial imaging is required; relapse is common, mandating extended follow-up. Watch for azole toxicities, including voriconazole-induced skeletal fluorosis with prolonged use.[5][7]
